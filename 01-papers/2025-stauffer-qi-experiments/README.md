**TL;DR:** A follow-up to the author's 2024 drive note, "ALMOST purely speculation and internet research". It reports one home experiment. A Newton's cradle was chilled overnight in a freezer, and two end balls were then heated in boiling water. If hotter balls had more inertia, the cold balls should bounce higher. "No such effects were visually observed." The main proposal is a vacuum centrifuge test, for example on a SpinLaunch machine: one arm carries a heated gas tank and the other a cooled one, and imbalance is measured by active spin-balancing. The paper also corrects the 2024 thruster idea, saying wall collisions probably cancel its thrust.

## Key claims
- Section "Last-Paper Errata and Embellishments" (pp. 4–5): in the 2024 thermal-gradient thruster, molecules hitting the side walls at angles would return the thrust gained at the hot end. "I will bet that it all balances out, with or without QI effects. … If QI theories are true, the whole scheme might still work. But it is far more complex than I had initially thought." (p. 5). A second erratum explains the 2024 search for magnetic gases: the idea was to throw mass out fast (high inertia) and reel it back slowly (low inertia).
- Section "Using a Newton's Cradle for Testing QI Theories" (pp. 6–7): describes the freezer-and-boiling-water test (null, by eye) and ideas for a larger instrumented version: vacuum, Peltier-heated balls, gas-filled balls, AI video analysis and EM shielding. The author is "frankly skeptical about the whole idea of testing QI theories with any version of a Newton's Cradle, for lack of sufficient precision" (p. 7).
- Section "Misc. Transition-Intermission, and Testing a QI Thruster in a Lab Setting" (pp. 8–11): tests must measure inertia, not weight. A lab thruster should hang horizontally in vacuum (to avoid convection), with interferometric readout. The EmDrive null tests by Tajmar's group are cited as the model.
- He reproduces chatbot (Perplexity) answers on force sensitivity, and on the mass-energy increase from heating (about 8.28 × 10⁻¹² kg for 1 kg of iron heated from 20 °C to its melting point, about 745 kJ; pp. 9–11). He suggests this E = mc² effect would add slightly to his thruster's thrust (p. 11), and later that it must be calculated so it is not confused with a QI signal (p. 16).
- Section "Testing QI Theories Using a Centrifuge" (pp. 11–18): balanced arms with heated vs cooled masses, varying the gas species, pressure and Peltier polarity to expose confounders. The imbalance would be measured by active rotor balancing (pumped fluid, magnetically positioned masses, SQUIDs or piezo sensors) rather than by raw force measurement, which he considers "hopeless" (p. 13). Listed pitfalls: density stratification of fluids under high g, "cogging torque" from motors and power transfer (so measure only while coasting, mechanically and thermally), and where to dump the pumped heat (a liquid loop between the two arms).
- Concluding remarks (pp. 18–19): knowing whether QI is real "is clearly of great importance"; "I, for one, am sure hoping that 'QI theories' (of Quantum Inertia) can be successfully tested!"

## Key equations
None of the author's own. The only formula is the quoted chatbot E = mc² estimate (p. 11). Editorial check: 745,000 J / (3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)² ≈ 8.3 × 10⁻¹² kg, which is arithmetically correct.

## Data used
- One informal home experiment: a consumer Newton's cradle, freezer-chilled, with two end balls heated in boiling water (p. 1). Observation was visual only, with no measurement or controls.
- References (p. 19): the author's 2024 note, three TU Dresden papers (Tajmar et al. 2021 EmDrive; Neunzig et al. 2021 laser resonators; Monette et al. 2021 Mach-effect thrusters), a 10⁻²⁸ N force-metrology preprint (arXiv:2208.05368), and a centrifuge-imbalance paper (Sensors 23, 3797). Background reading suggested: NextBigFuture and quantizedinertia.com/about (p. 3).

## Results & predictions
- **Result:** null (by eye) in the Newton's cradle test. The author notes the effect "MIGHT have been there, but were too small to observe" (p. 1).
- **No quantitative prediction** for any proposed experiment.
- **Editorial check (our reading):** as for the 2024 note, QI's published factor (1 − 2c²/aΘ) differs from 1 by only about 10⁻²⁵ at the accelerations of colliding molecules or balls (≫ 1 m s⁻²). So McCulloch's formula predicts no temperature-dependent inertia detectable by these methods. The null cradle result therefore does not test QI as published. The proposed experiments test the author's interpretation (hot matter has more inertia), not QI's horizon mechanism.
